I want to start with a short story, one that shaped how I think about failure.
A few years ago, I was working on a project that I was absolutely certain would succeed.
I had planned every detail, checked every assumption, and prepared for every scenario I could imagine.
And yet, within the first month, almost everything went wrong.
The technology did not behave the way we expected.
Our timeline collapsed, our budget disappeared, and morale on the team dropped sharply.
At the time, it felt like the end of something important.
Looking back now, I can say with confidence that it was actually the beginning.
Because that failure forced us to ask questions we had never bothered to ask before.
We stopped assuming we already knew the answers, and we started genuinely listening.
We spoke to the people who would actually use what we were building.
We threw away our original plan and built something much simpler instead.
It was not glamorous, and it was not what we had originally envisioned.
But it worked, and more importantly, people actually wanted to use it.
I tell this story because I think we misunderstand failure in a fundamental way.
We are taught, from a very young age, to avoid mistakes at almost any cost.
Schools reward correct answers, and they often quietly punish honest uncertainty.
By the time we reach university or our first job, many of us are afraid to be wrong.
But if you look closely at almost any meaningful achievement, failure is right there beneath it.
Every scientist who ever made a discovery ran experiments that did not work first.
Every engineer who ever built something reliable broke several earlier versions along the way.
Every writer who ever finished a good book wrote pages they eventually deleted.
Failure, in other words, is not the opposite of success.
It is usually a necessary and unavoidable part of the process that leads to it.
So how should we actually respond when something we tried does not work?
The first step, I believe, is to separate the outcome from your own self-worth.
A failed experiment does not mean you are a failure as a person.
It simply means you now have information that you did not have before.
The second step is to ask specific, honest questions about what actually happened.
Not vague questions like why did this fail, but precise ones about each decision.
What assumption turned out to be wrong, and why did we believe it in the first place?
The third step is to share what you learned, rather than quietly hiding it.
Many of the best lessons in any field come from mistakes that were openly discussed.
When we hide our failures, we force other people to repeat them unnecessarily.
When we share them honestly, we accelerate progress for everyone around us.
None of this means we should seek out failure for its own sake.
We should still plan carefully, and we should still aim to succeed.
But we should hold our plans a little more loosely than we tend to.
We should expect that reality will surprise us, because it almost always does.
And we should build enough flexibility into our work to adapt when it does.
This mindset matters especially for anyone working at the edge of what is known.
If you are doing research, or building something genuinely new, uncertainty is not optional.
You cannot predict everything in advance, no matter how carefully you plan.
The people who thrive in these conditions are not the ones who never fail.
They are the ones who recover quickly, learn honestly, and keep moving forward.
As you continue through your studies and into your careers, you will fail sometimes.
An experiment will not work, a project will slip, a plan will fall apart.
When that happens, I hope you remember that this is not the end of the story.
It is simply data, and data can always guide you toward a better next attempt.
Treat your failures with the same curiosity you bring to your successes.
That, more than any single skill, is what will carry you furthest over time.
Thank you very much for listening, and I wish you every success ahead.
