I would like to spend the next few minutes talking about artificial intelligence.
It is a term we hear constantly, in the news and in everyday conversation.
But what does it actually mean, and how does it really work?
At its core, artificial intelligence is about building systems that can learn from data.
Instead of following a fixed set of rules written by a programmer, these systems adapt.
They look at many examples, and they gradually improve their own performance over time.
This approach is often called machine learning, and it sits at the heart of modern AI.
Let's break this down into a few major categories that you may have heard about.
The first category is supervised learning, which is probably the most common approach today.
In supervised learning, a model is trained on labelled examples, like photos tagged with categories.
Over many repetitions, the model learns to predict the correct label for new, unseen examples.
The second category is unsupervised learning, where there are no labels provided at all.
Here, the system tries to find hidden patterns or groupings within the data on its own.
The third category is reinforcement learning, inspired loosely by how humans and animals learn.
An agent takes actions in an environment and receives rewards or penalties based on the outcome.
Over time, it learns a strategy that maximizes its long-term reward.
This is the same technique used to train systems that can play complex games at a high level.
Now, you may be wondering why artificial intelligence has become so powerful in recent years.
Three things came together almost at the same time to make this possible.
First, we now have access to enormous amounts of digital data to learn from.
Second, computing hardware, especially graphics processors, became dramatically faster and cheaper.
Third, researchers developed better algorithms, particularly a family of models called neural networks.
Neural networks are loosely inspired by the structure of the human brain.
They consist of layers of simple units that pass information to one another.
Individually, each unit performs a very simple calculation.
But when you stack millions of them together, the system can learn remarkably complex behavior.
This is the technology behind image recognition, translation, and modern voice assistants.
It is also the technology behind the large language models you may have used yourself.
Of course, artificial intelligence is not without its risks and its open questions.
Models can inherit biases that were present in their training data.
They can also make confident mistakes, which can be difficult to detect in practice.
There are important questions about privacy, about fairness, and about accountability.
Who is responsible when an automated system makes a harmful decision?
These are not just technical questions, they are also deeply social and ethical ones.
Researchers, companies, and governments are all working, at different speeds, to address them.
Regulation is beginning to catch up with the pace of technological change.
Transparency and explainability have become major topics of active research.
We want systems whose decisions we can actually understand and question.
Despite these challenges, the potential benefits of artificial intelligence remain enormous.
In medicine, models can help detect diseases earlier than traditional methods allow.
In science, they can accelerate discovery by exploring possibilities far faster than humans can.
In everyday life, they can make technology more accessible to people with disabilities.
The key, I believe, is not to fear this technology, nor to blindly trust it.
Instead, we should aim to understand it well enough to use it responsibly.
That means asking good questions about how systems are built and evaluated.
It means demanding transparency from the organizations that deploy them at scale.
And it means remembering that these are tools, built by people, for people.
As students and future engineers, you are in a unique position.
You will not just use this technology, many of you will help build it.
That comes with real responsibility, but also with real opportunity.
I encourage you to stay curious, stay critical, and keep asking how and why.
Artificial intelligence will keep evolving quickly over the next decade.
How it shapes our world will depend, in large part, on the choices we make now.
Thank you again for your attention, and I look forward to your questions.
